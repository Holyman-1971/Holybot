Thing is, [b]*NO*[/b] money is real, whether it is credit, debt, currency or bond.

Money is just a notional, abstract concept that humans use as a medium of exchange and a store of value.

Currency is money tokenised: the abstract made construct.

We are all aware that currency tokens - particularly paper money - have no intrinsic value, save for the value that they represent (a value that variously fluctuates in any case). This hasn't always been the case: early monetary tokens (coin) held precisely their value in their (precious) metallic composition. The minting of coins that carried the imprimatur of government treasury or ruling Head of State were meant to guarantee the gold or silver content of the coin.

But this practice declined gradually, until it was completely displaced by [i]fiat[/i] currency, following the abolition of the Gold Standard.

Today, the implicit value of a monetary token - currency - is agreed, somewhat informally, by those who agree to use such tokens as a medium of exchange. Different currencies inspire different levels of confidence, for example the difference between a current U.S. Dollar note and a Zimbabwean (billion...) Dollar note.

But increasingly, money exchanges take place electronically, with no actual physical transfer of currency tokens.

And here's where we begin to move into the realms of today's Global Economic problems.

As I explained in a previous post to SomeGuy, Capitalism as an Economic System has moved through various iterations in its History.

It's not actually helpful to think of Capitalism as some kind of ideology or Economic Philosophical Principle, because there has never been a full and systemic codification of exactly what Capitalism is. "Capitalism" is essentially a synonym for an Economic System that is founded upon the flow and exchange of value between Humans and their Society.

Communism is not Capitalism's ideological opposite; Communism [b]*IS A FORM OF CAPITALISM*[/b]. The distinction between Marxist or Soviet Communist Capitalism and the brand or flavour of Capitalism dominant in Western Societies, is simply a question of ownership. In Western Capitalism, land, property, the means of production, produce, product and (latterly) social infrastructure are all privately owned; with Communist Capitalism, ownership of the same is held in common trust by the State or Central Government.

Flows of value and the exchange of monies take place in exactly the same way, just that in Communist Capitalism the beneficiaries of surplus and profit are (theoretically...) "The People [sic]"; and in Western Capitalism, private individuals or corporations.

So try to think of Capitalism as purely an Economic (non-political, non-ideological) System supporting the flow and exchange of money (Capital) amongst humans and their societies.

Okay, now Capitalism has been through three distinct phases (and numerous minor iterations and regional variations) in its History.

A fourth, proto-phase of Capitalism could be argued to have existed in the Classical Greco-Roman World (and to a lesser extent in its Eastern Contemporary, Imperial China) - but I'll not bother with that argument here.

The (inarguably) first phase of Capitalism began in the Islamic World in the 9th Century C.E., and is called Merchant Capitalism.

This was Capitalism in perhaps its purest form, in that money took second place to merchandise. Although it was fuelled by an aspiration for profit, real value in the Merchant Capitalism system was always primarily stored in the merchandise or produce, rather than in any medium of exchange used for transaction. Indeed, since the primary medium of exchange was precious metal (gold, silver etc.), the medium of exchange was as much a mercantile commodity as the merchandise and produce.

The power players in Merchant Capitalism weren't so much the people who "owned" the merchandise and produce; so much as it was those who could preserve and protect [b]*POSSESSION*[/b] of commodities. In other words, it was the Kings, Queens, Sultans, Emperors and Shahs who had control of large security forces which could protect merchants and trade caravans, as well as market places and treasuries, who were the real, uh, King-pins.

Hence the correlation between Merchant Capitalism and Feudalism.

The second phase of Capitalism is called Industrial Capitalism.

Beginning with the Industrial Revolution in the 18th Century, Industrial Capitalism heralded a much more complex division of labour, primarily due to the rise of the factory system, which de-emphasised the role of skilled artisans and tradesmen. This meant that the most powerful players ceased to be those individuals capable of (themselves) producing merchandise, or those who owned merchandise; and became those who owned the [b]*MEANS*[/b] of producing merchandise.

As the name implies then, during the Industrial Capitalism phase, the most powerful (wealthy) individuals were the Industrialists. These were individuals who privately owned the means of production, and controlled (and frequently exploited) the human labour required to operate the factories where everything was produced. This period also saw the Industrialists become owners of significant areas of land and property, as the moribund aristocracy were forced to sell their ancestral land-rights in order to keep pace with the New World Order.

As technological innovation and the competition it created increased during the 20th Century, the requirement for Industrialists to invest in new technology and equipment laid the foundations for the shift into the third (and final?) phase of Capitalism: Finance Capitalism.

Interestingly enough, mid-way through the 20th Century, both Fascist and Communist Governments were very vocal in their opposition to Finance Capitalism, since (presciently) both ideologies recognised the threat that Finance Capitalism posed to the power of the State.

Finance Capitalism shifted the power-base from the owners (or regulators) of tangible assets and means of production, to those who had mastered the arcane mysteries of Financial Instruments such as stocks, credit and debit derivatives.

Financiers and Bankers could make money appear from out of thin air. The concept (or "miracle") of compound interest meant that those capable of lending money, could make easy money, without needing to lift much more than the finger used to sign a Futures Contract, or latterly, punch the "Enter" key on a complex derivatives exchange.
