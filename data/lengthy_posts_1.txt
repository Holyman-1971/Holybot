"That lowbrow's comments..."

Ah, I think it's the idiomatic nature of my prose.

The sentence was:

"It might stop the low-brows bitching and whining about lengthy posts (that they�re really not required to read)."

Now that could be read as meaning the bitching and whining that belongs to one or more low-brows; in which case your use of the singular or plural possessive apostrophe would be correct.

But I actually meant it to mean that the lowbrows have been bitching and whining, and that what ever it was I was talking about, might cause the low-brows to cease the act of bitching and whining.

Technically speaking, there's no way for me to stop the bitching and whining that belongs to the low-brows - because it belongs to them, and only they can control it.

But since their specific bitching and whining about the length of my posts is prompted by an action on my part (writing lengthy posts), my curtailment of said action would cause a corresponding cessation in their actions. To whit: end their act of bitching and whining about the length of my posts.

Of course, had I used the singular low-brow, rather than low-brows, then it would have been as an adjective, rather than a noun. Low-brow would have been descriptive of the bitching and whining itself, rather than the individuals doing the bitching and whining.

But I figured the bitching and whining wasn't the sole dominion of the low-brow's - I'd been doing a fair bit of it myself, particularly in response to you, ahem, The Talking Walnut.

So I meant that I might stop, the group of people I was referring to as low-brows, from the act of bitching and whining about the length of my posts; rather than actually stop the bitching and whining that actually (alliteration�) belongs to those individuals, and about which I am powerless to do anything.
