"The Charge of the Light Brigade" was a milestone in Military History, not because of the number of lives lost, but because it marked an irreversible change in the conduct of War.

From the 17th to the early 19th Century, �The Age of Battles�, War was seen as a Noble Game. By and large, the conduct of individual, set-piece battles was a �civilised� affair; carried out by noblemen who, more often than not were related, or who at least were acquainted through European Court Society. They almost certainly learned the �Art of War� at the same establishments.

Warfare during �The Age of Battles� was a straightforward affair, with forces divided cleanly into 3 groups: infantry; cavalry; and artillery. These groups further sub-divided into nothing more than �Light� or �Heavy� categories or divisions.

The only time that civilians ever became unwillingly involved in warfare, was when they chose to remain within a besieged city � they were generally permitted to leave as the siege was established. Once the breach in the city�s wall was made by artillery, and from the moment that �The Forlorn Hope� stormed that breach, any civilians remaining within the city were considered �fair game�, at least for a day or two, by the successfully invading force.

But by the mid-19th Century, the effects of the Industrial Revolution were beginning to be felt on the battlefields of Europe. Still a few years before the invention of the automatic machine-gun, rifling techniques and breech-loading had increased the range and accuracy of infantry weapons. And improvements in casting, metallurgy and chemistry had significantly enhanced the power and effectiveness of artillery.

The horse though, was still a horse. It wouldn�t be until the invention of the tank towards the end of World War I, and later still, the invention of the helicopter, that the tactical equivalent of cavalry would catch-up with the advances in artillery and infantry weapons.

The cavalry was the most prestigious branch of the military. As far as Society was concerned, a dashing cavalry captain was a greater asset to a Society function, than a swarthy infantryman, or soot-covered (and usually deaf) artilleryman. �The Cavalry� was, and to a degree in the UK still is, the place in the Army for the sons of nobility and gentlemen.

�Horseback Warriors� in 19th Century continued the tradition and romance of the �Knights of Yore�. It required a skill in horse-riding that often came only from the facility to own a horse. And it was on that black day in Balaclava, 150 years ago, that the nobility and romance of the Cavalry, came face to face with the exigencies of modern warfare. A steely nerve, undoubted courage, and an almost complete inability to face the Truth, met the unavoidable reality of modern technology transposed to the battlefield.

The Charge of the Light Brigade wasn�t complete carnage; it didn�t destroy an entire regiment or a whole section of society; but it did mark the point at which people stopped playing games on the Battlefield. It was the point at which War as a past-time, as a political efficacy, became an industrial process. War became as much a contributor to the political and economic strength of a country, as its cotton mills and factories.

War ceased to be an Art, or a Game, and became instead a process for demonstrating the ruthless efficiency and resolve of a Nation or Empire.
