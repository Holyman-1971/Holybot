Tell you what, here's some non-partisan, objective History about military uniforms, and I'll see where I go from there! :)

All males fight.

Doesn't matter what species they are, fighting is what males do. They do it to defend their mating rights, and their feeding territory. Not much else. And since the Human feeding territory is pretty much completely collective, that only leaves mating rights as the reason why male humans fight. And "Defending Family & Loved Ones" is crucial to protecting a male's mating fights...

Anyhoo, point is, fighting is terribly destructive. Even the male lion that wins a fight, will be so weakened by the encounter, that for a while after, he is susceptible to defeat by an otherwise weaker opponent.

So the real trick to fighting, is to win the fight without actually having to fight. And nature and Human culture have developed many ways to try and do this.

Nature equips its animals with fearsome looking tusks, teeth, and manes, and just-as-fearsome sounding roars, snarls and growls. And of course, in the far more delicate Avian world, nature equips its male birds with a fearsome (to other birds) display of feathers, of which the pea-cock is the prime example.

The idea is that these visual and audible cues, actually prevent a "fight" from taking place, by making the less fearsome looking/sounding male back down. Of course it isn't a perfect system, and nature does have a tendency to be red in tooth and claw and all that. But on the whole, in the Natural World, the idea is to preserve energy, not destroy it, especially one's own.

Human culture though, has developed the Art of Aggressive Displays, to a much higher and collective level. But the truth remains that one Human individual or collective would rather frighten the enemy into withdrawing in submission, than actually fight them into doing same.

Which brings us to military uniforms.

With 1st and 2nd Generation Warfare (1GW & 2GW), the idea was that the Feudal Lord/Aristo'/King would turn up on the Battlefield, and array his army against the army of the foe. The idea was to intimidate the enemy into withdrawing, without a shot being fired, a sword being sliced, or a horse charged.

In order to facilitate this, the Army was designed through its formations, weapons and eventually (2GW) uniform, to look as frightening, imposing, and undefeatable as possible. The idea being to make the enemy shit its pants, and run home without the need for any bloodshed.

It was for this reason that the national armies of the "Age of Battles" (c. 17th Century to c. 19th Century), were all dressed up in bright and vivid colours. The English, as most Anglophones know, wore red coats; the French wore blue; the Prussians wore black; the Spanish yellow; the Portugese Green(-ish, with a splash of red); the Austro-Hungarians (Holy Roman's...) wore white; and the Russians... Well, I can't remember what colour they wore...

The idea then, with 2nd Generation Warfare, was not to conceal and camouflage one's troops; but to present and position them to create such an awesome display of killing ability, that the enemy would scurry back off to whatever hole they had emerged from.

Finding one's self actually face-to-face with British Redcoats, would remind one of the fearsome reputation of the Redcoats' volley-fire. Watching a blue-coated column of the French Imperial Guard marching up the hill towards you, would remind you that Napoleon's columns had never been beaten in the European Theatre of 19th Century War. Well, not until they came up against the British Redcoats in the Peninsular War, and of course at Waterloo.

But it was at the beginning of the 19th Century that the technology emerging from the Industrial Revolution began to change the whole dynamic. The introduction of the breech-loading, rifled musket, with greater accuracy and ease-of-use, as well as improvements to artillery pieces, meant that lining your men up to be shot at, was proving less and less successful.

The British were the first of the Great Nations to introduce Rifle Regiments into the Line - and anyone familiar with Bernard Cornwell's "Sharpe" series of novels, and the Sean Bean TV films based on them will already know this. These British Light Infantry batallions were the first soldiers to be issued with a uniform that was designed to conceal them. It wasn't quite disruptive pattern material (DPM), but the green jackets they wore certainly camouflaged them more than the bright and shiny red jackets (with sparkling white accoutrements) worn by their colleagues in Regiments of the Line.

These "Royal Green Jackets" (amongst other LI regiments) then were intended to function as skirmishers, working in pairs, in the No-Man's-Land between the opposing lines. Naturally Napoleon caught onto this and produced his own skirmishers in the shape of "Voltigeurs", who wore a darker blue than the Imperial blue of the soldiers of the Line.

And it all really developed from there.

By the time we get to 3rd Generation Warfare (3GW) at the beginning of the 20th Century, the vast majority of battlefield casualties were being produced not by one-to-one soldier combat; but by targeting those soldiers with increasingly effective, industrialised human-mincing machines, in the form of static machine-guns and artillery barrages. And of course later, aerial bombardment from balloons and planes.

And so at this point, whilst it remained vital to land warfare to have as big an infantry presence as possible in the field; it was even more vital to camouflage and conceal them.

And it's 3GW all the way from World War One through to the end of World War Two.

No-one really agrees about the exact moment that 3rd Generation Warfare gave way to 4th Generation Warfare (4GW); though the American invasion of Vietnam, and the subsequent War is generally accepted as the tipping-point.

With 4th Generation Warfare, it made sense for the camouflage and concealment of soldiers to be taken to its logical extreme, and make them indistinguishable from non-combatants.

The Allies had pretty much torn-up the idea of not targeting civilians in War - an informal protocol that had existed pretty much throughout the History of Warfare (excepting the permitted ransacking and pillaging of sieged cities) - through their campaign of aerial bombardment of Germany and Japan, and of course, with the use of nuclear weapons against civilian populations. So when it came to smaller, weaker "Armies" defending their homelands against vastly superior conventional (3GW) military forces, resistance fighters, guerillas, insurgents, whatever-you-want-to-call them, figured that it didn't make any difference whether they looked like civilians or not.

And besides, contrary to received opinion, it isn't a uniform that indicates whether someone is a soldier or not; it is whether or not they happen to be carrying a weapon that is the real giveaway.

There's a fairly simple formula:

Human + Weapon = Fighter

Human + No Weapon = Not a Fighter; or not currently fighting (same difference)

Actually, bitching about whether the enemy is wearing an identifiable uniform or not is a farily naive position to take. When a tin-pot dictator, or rag-tag collection of resistance fighters/insurgents/whatever, puts an fighting force together, the first consideration (these days anyway), is not how splendid they will look - how shiny their belts, boots and buckles are - but how well armed they are.

Of course, in the Developed World, there is enough money to equip the military with both weapons and shiny uniforms. But most of the people that the Developed World insists on fighting, aren't quite so well appointed. So it comes down to a choice between easily indentifiable, but unarmed soldiers; or arming to the teeth, individuals that are indistinguishable from the civilian population.

But again, the rule of thumb to apply, is to not consider what people look like, but to think about whether or not they are carrying a weapon. And if they're pointing that weapon at you, chances are they are your enemy, particularly if the weapon is making loud bangs and sending red-hot pieces of metal towards you at supersonic speeds.

There we go then. A not-at-all concise treatise on the nature of War, and the self-destructive combination of Humanity's bellicosity and naivety.
