It�s mostly to do with the murky world of macroeconomics.

The U.S. dollar, like the British Pound and essentially, the European Euro is a �Fiat� currency.  HYPERLINK "http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiat_currency" http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiat_currency Very, very simply, a fiat currency has value because of the perceived �value� of the country (government) that issues it. So a country like the United States can use a fiat currency, because, well, it�s the United States, No. 1 power and all that. A developing or Third World country that wishes to issue its own currency, on the other hand, would need to back it with an internationally tradable commodity, usually gold, though other precious metals and minerals may also serve as the currency �standard�.

So, the idea is that the U.S. dollar is hard currency, pretty much anywhere in the World,  not because it is backed by a gold, or even direct oil standard, but because, well, the United States is never going to go bankrupt, is it?

It is this fiat arrangement that has seen the U.S. dollar become not only the sole currency in use for international oil trading (and we all know how important oil is to any country in the 21st Century World), but also the de facto reserve currency for every major national economy.  HYPERLINK "http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency" http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reserve_currency

All well and good so far.

But here�s the thing. The United States Federal Reserve (a privately-owned banking institution) can issue as much dollar currency as it sees fit. The actual *value* of that dollar fiat currency is determined not by how much gold the Federal Reserve owns, but by the value of the dollar relative to other World currencies. In other words, the value of the dollar is determined by how valuable it is to countries other than the United States of America.

And of course, since U.S. dollars are the *only* currency with which any nation can buy oil, the currency is seen as pretty damn valuable, by near damn everyone.

And that�s pretty much the (pretty simplified) state of play today.

So what happens if it becomes possible to buy oil in a currency other than the U.S. dollar?

Well first things first, the U.S. dollar loses the innate value that comes from it being the only currency accepted in international oil trades. This presents a two-fold problem.

The first is that the drop in value of the U.S. dollar means a drop in the value of most countries� foreign-exchange reserves. And countries, just like people, don�t like to have less money. So, before the value of the dollar drops too far, countries holding U.S. dollars in their foreign-exchange, start to sell them, and replace them with another currency or currencies that still hold value, for example the Euro, or increasingly, the Chinese Renminbi.

As you might know if you�ve ever bought stocks or shares, or have studied the History of the Wall Street Crash, pretty soon the U.S. dollar faces a �run� on its value, as non-U.S. holders of the dollar seek to sell them before they become completely worthless.

And that connects with the secondary issue.

Imagine I wrote you a cheque for say US$100, in exchange for some goods or services you have supplied me. Ordinarily, you would immediately cash that cheque. However, just last month, I wrote another cheque for US$50 to somebody else; that someone else also has something that you want to buy, for let�s say US$50. So, rather than cash your $100 cheque from me, you give it to that other person, in exchange for what you want, plus their US$50 cheque. And so on.

Now all the time you and others are trading with each other, using my cheques as currency, those cheques are never presented to my bank account. However, just as soon as it is no longer so useful for you to keep my cheques for your own trading, you�ll pay them in and they�ll be presented against my bank account. As soon as that perceived value diminishes or disappears however, it becomes an enormous problem, a problem big enough to make it worth going to War over.

Now you might ask why so many countries that are politically and diplomatically opposed to the United States � primarily China, of course � continue to support the fiat value of the USD.

The answer is simply that the bankruptcy of the U.S. Economy would plunge the whole World into serious, possibly catastrophic economic decline. Most macroeconomists estimate that such a decline would make the Wall Street Crash and the World-wide depression of the 1930�s look like a minor overdraft infringement. This is primarily because of the much greater integration of the World-wide economy of the 21st Century.

However, there is an event horizon � and it is fast approaching � where for a country to *not* bail from the increasingly worthless and debt-ridden U.S. economy would mean its going down the tubes along with the United States. If the country (and countries) pull out � effectively disengage from the USD as reserve currency � before the dollar hits the skids, then things will be marginally less worse for them. In short, if they stick with the US dollar up to the point of bankruptcy, they will be bankrupt themselves. If they pull out before bankruptcy, then they will just be very, very poor, along with every other country, certainly in the Northern Hemisphere, except the United States, which will of course be bankrupt and destitute.
