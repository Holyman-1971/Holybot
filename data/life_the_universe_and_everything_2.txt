I think that our justice system already does take account of the absence of Free Will in people's actions, to a limited degree at least.

Think about sentencing. Where mandatory sentencing is not the case, a judge or jury will deliberate on the most appropriate sentence for a given crime. And usually, that comes down to whether or not the individuals passing sentence can relate to the reason why the convicted person committed the crime. In other words, whether the reason why the crime was committed or not was "understandable".

That used to be a standard defence in many crimes, including murder, a so-called [i]crime de passionelle[/i] - "A crime of passion". Did the person commit the crime because they were in the grip of an emotional fugue? Did they just do what their passions and instincts compelled them to do? Did they just do what anyone of us would do in the circumstances?

In this sense, justice is less concerned with whether a person wilfully chose to do something they knew to be wrong, and more concerned with whether a person is capable of [b]RESTRAINING[/b] those criminal compulsions we all feel at one time or another.

In other words, we might all [b]FEEL[/b] like committing a certain crime given certain provocations, but law-abiding citizens have the strength to restrain such impulses and not act upon them. At which point we find we are punishing people not because of the way they chose to exercise their Free Will, but because they are too weak to overcome the criminal tendencies that are inherent to the Human Being in Society.

I don't think that is any great revelation. It may not be fully stated or accepted that this is the foundation of Justice, but if you think about how we determine people to be of sufficient age to be liable for crimes - 10 years old, 18 years old, 21 years old etc. - we are saying that at those ages we expect Society's citizens to be responsible for their actions.

So Justice isn't really about Free Will, or holding people responsible for how they exercise their Free Will, but about holding people responsible for how well they manage their anti-social tendencies.

[quote=VigilanteGandi][color=green]There truly is a lot of evidence that as we live our lives each day our sub-conscious (or whatever you want to call it) mind constantly evaluates and makes decisions that best suit us.

I think you put it well in that while we believe that we make decisions based on our own thoughts and free will, most of what we do is just reactionary.[/color][/quote]

Have you seen "The Matrix" Trilogy?

In the scene where Neo is talking to The Architect, the Architect explains how previous versions of the Matrix failed because of a systemic flaw that the Architect could not eradicate because he couldn't understand it. He eventually set the intuitive program called "The Oracle" to working out the problem, and She came up with the answer that the humans plugged into the Matrix needed to consciously believe that they had a Choice. The Architect then set about creating the [b]ILLUSION[/b] of Choice, and this largely resolved the issue (though still with enough room for Neo to work his magic and the film to have a point...).

So this begs that we all ask ourselves the question: when, exactly, in our lives, do we ever exactly exercise Choice? When, exactly, do we exercise our Free Will?

Isn't it the case that when faced with a decision in our lives, rather than making a genuinely free choice, we actually try to work out exactly what is in our best interests, and go with the "Choice" that we think best serves those interests?

That's not really choice or Free Will: that is our instinct for survival at work.

But what about choices that have no direct bearing on our survival? Mint-choc-chip ice cream, or cookie-dough, for example? The red car or the blue car? The Simpsons or South Park?

Are these decisions where we are really exercising our Free Will, or are they "decisions" where the answer is already established by everything that has previously happened to us in our Life Experience, and we just go through a self-deluding charade in order to create an illusion that we are exercising our Free Will?

And perhaps more pertinently: what about the constant bombardment of advertising, marketing, politicking and peer-pressure that seeks to shape, channel and condition the choices we make in and about our lives? Are these all for nought? Are the billions spent on advertising wasted money, or does advertising actually work?

I don't think that the challenge is to prove that Free Will doesn't exist; I think it is to prove that it [b]DOES[/b] exist.
