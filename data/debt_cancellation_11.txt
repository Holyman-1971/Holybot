As a commodity, gold is a somewhat special case, and the dynamics that apply to it are very different to those which apply to every other commodity.

If gold were *JUST* another utility commodity, like petrol, iron-ore, wheat &c., it would behave like any other commodity. But whilst it does have its practical/industrial applications, gold holds a very special place in Human Society, a place like no other.

In and of itself, gold has never had any intrinsic value, not like say food, coal or wool. Only very recently with the rise of electronic machinery has any practical purpose really been found for it. Other than that though, gold has value because... Everyone *AGREES* it has value.

Of course gold doesn't decay, rust or tarnish (unlike silver and (eventually) platinum)), and it is sufficiently malleable to be used for delicate filigree work. And it is perhaps this incorruptibility that makes it appeal to human beings. But mostly, gold is just a nice shiny metal.

Most speculative bubbles are based on the idea that something is going to be worth a lot more soon, because everyone will want or need more of it sooner rather than later, for one reason or another.

But the main reason why demand for gold spikes upwards, is because people are losing confidence in [i]fiat[/i] currencies, stocks and shares, and the value of other commodities.

So like any investment, making money from gold is just about buying low and selling high.

But as an indicator for confidence levels in the rest of the Economy, and financial confidence within Society as a whole: nothing beats Gold.

How about using it to buy tools and equipment to make you as self-sufficient as possible?

No, I'm not going to suggest that Society may break down and you'll need to be able to generate your own electricity and clean water...

But if you think about the bulk of what money you do spend, chances are it goes on necessities like electricity, gas, food, repairs, clothing etc.

People often ask, "Is it better to get a mortgage or rent?"

The correct answer is: neither. It is better to live in a home that you don't have to pay anyone else anything at all to live in.

The response to that is then usually: "Well yes, of course, but who can afford to do that?"

And the answer to that is: virtually everyone.

The answer to the question: "Who can afford to live in the kind of house they really want to live in?"... Is usually: no-one.

But if you look at what you could afford to buy without borrowing significant amounts of money from anyone, chances are there will be something, somewhere. It might not be the kind of home you feel suits your socio-economic status; and it might not be located within a reasonable commute from your place of work. But there will be a home somewhere, which you could afford to buy with at most, only a little short-term borrowing.

Once you've got a home (or are well on your way to getting a home) that you can truly call your own, well you can go back to renting or buying another place, just as you were. If your own (actually) owned home doesn't cost you anything in rent or loan repayments, then you're free to carry on as before. Except you will [i]always[/i] have a home to go back to, if your personal economic situation changes.

The best investment we can make with our surplus cash, is anything that helps increase our self-sufficiency and reduces our dependence on others.

Items that we consume and discard, don't really fit that bill.
