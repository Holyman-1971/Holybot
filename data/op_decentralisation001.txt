A tangled web of bureaucracies and elected offices (and competing things such as regional offices) creates a veritable nightmare in terms of governance. Especially if all those components are allowed any degree of independent agendas.[/color][/quote]

I think the main issue with governments and their bureaucracies is centralisation.

Not so much with specific functions, departments and portfolios, but in the way that everything is centralised under a single government.

We all have different issues and priorities in our lives. Who we vote in to take responsibility for the messy business of providing social governance, is usually a reflection of those different issues and priorities.

Usually however, we only have the opportunity to vote for a single individual or party of ideologically-aligned individuals to govern and manage... Everything.

This is because it is accepted wisdom that a single individual ought to govern and manage Everything.

The result is that we have Healthcare priorities competing with the Banking Sector for financial support; as well as agriculture competing with manufacturing, mass transit competing with defence, and education needs competing with justice departments.

These competitions combined with finite material resources and political priorities have driven the creation of sprawling bureaucracies. These bureaucracies are intended to control and monitor how central government funding is distributed among the various functions it is mandated to deliver. However, such bureaucracies invariably impede the delivery of services and rarely deliver the anticipated and required levels of accountability.

All because of the standard practice of Centralisation.

Decentralisation of control and funding for essential social functions allows for greater flexibility in delivery of services; and greater accountability of those elected to manage responsibility for each service.

Transition to a decentralised model can happen incrementally and as gradually as required for each function. Should the transition of a particular service function encounter problems, the process can be slowed or fully rolled-back (to central government control...).

[quote=SomeGuy post_id=5804 time=1683827405 user_id=73][color=#00BF00]Historically many of the most effective forms of government have been extremely simple such as military dictatorships and absolute monarchies. They frequently also prove the most abusive and/or evil.[/color][/quote]

It is a double-edged sword.

Military dictators and absolute monarchs can unilaterally decide who gets what, and how each function operates. This generally negates the need for any bureaucracies, which when coupled with fear of the dictator/monarch/tyrant, leads to a noticeable improvement in services.

However, like you say, such "Leaders" often fall victim to their own mythology and frequently become abusive, paranoid and inefficient. If the centralisation of authority in a government of professional politicians is problematic, centralising authority in a single individual usually proves disastrous.

[quote=SomeGuy post_id=5804 time=1683827405 user_id=73][color=#00BF00]The solution is a limited form of government. Specifically one which has only just enough power to solve the most necessary issues such as building roads, having a military, keeping businesses in check and so on. But at the same time has a very tight leash on what it can do beyond that. Little or no ability to create and execute agendas beyond elected offices, tight systems and rules for accountability and specific defined limitations to its power enforced by a combination of the aforementioned accountability and the people.[/color][/quote]
