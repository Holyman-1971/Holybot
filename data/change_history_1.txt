OK, if I could change a single moment in History, I think it would be to persuade Julius Caesar *NOT* to cross the Rubicon River with his legions in 49 B.C.E.

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubicon

If I had been there, I would have tried to explain to Caesar that by crossing the Rubicon, he would not only lay the foundations for his own assassination 5 years later, but would also set the cause of Human Democracy back by a good 1,500 years or so.

Democracy began in the Greek City States, 400 years or so before it reached its (first/Classical-Era) zenith in the Roman Republic.

Had Caesar not created the conditions for his dictatorship (though that was a more benign position than we associate the term with these days), then he probably would have avoided assassination, there would have been no Civil War between Mark Anthony and Octavian, and the later would have been unlikely to establish himself as first Imperator of a Roman Empire.

The Roman Empire established the precedent for a militarily created and maintained Empire in the Western Hemisphere. The Empire first created the conditions for religious intolerance, before adopting Christianity as its sole religion, and making the Roman Catholic Church one of the most intolerant and controlling of all religions, plunging Europe into 1,000 years of theocratically enforced social and philosophical stagnation.

Had Roman Society remained "democratic" (in the same way that the United States Republic was "democratic" when it was founded: i.e. no votes for women or slaves), it is likely that Europe could have avoided the Dark Ages, and built upon the intellectual and philosophical foundations laid down by the Greeks and early Romans.

Instead, Europe had to wait 1,500 years for the Reformation, and the Renaissance, before it could continue the Human Social development that began in the Classical Era.

Had Caesar not crossed the Rubicon with his legions, there would have been no Roman Empire. Without a Roman Empire, there would have been no template for subsequent Imperially-minded dictators, all the way through to Napoleon and Hitler. Democracy could have flourished, and we could well have reached the heights of human sophistication that we are now at, much earlier in our History.

Of course, tweaking and changing History is merely an intellectual exercise, and virtually impossible to avoid creating a whole host of other problems.

Had there been no Roman Empire, without its religious and political intolerance, then a certain seditious Judean carpenter would not have been crucified (the Roman punishment for sedition) - though he may have been stoned to death (the Jewish punishment for blasphemy). The apparatus of the crumbling Roman Empire in the 4th Century C.E. would not have been transposed to the Roman Catholic Church. Christianity would likely have remained just another Jewish cult.

Some people may see that as *NOT* a good thing...

And of course, we have to consider that had Caesar not crossed the Rubicon, then perhaps someone else would have. So it's all a big "What if..?"

But if we're dealing with idealism here, and just making a single change that we can guarantee will not be subverted, then that would be the change I would make.
