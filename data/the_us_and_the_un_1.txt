In the United States, Local Government typically has at least two separate tiers: counties (parishes in Louisiana, and boroughs in Alaska); and municipalities (cities, towns, boroughs and villages).

There are also often local or regional "special districts" that exist for specific purposes, such as to provide fire protection, transit and water services and resources.

The average U.S. county population is about 100,00; though the most heavily populated county in the U.S. is Los Angeles County, which has a population just shy of 10 million citizens; which is a larger population than all but eight states of the Union.

Counties are usually governed by an elected board of supervisors, county commission, county freeholders, county council, or county legislature. A municipality is typically governed by a mayor and a city or municipal council. Broadly speaking, these are the lowest level of democratic Government, with an elected body representing the people of the county or municipality.

Due to the democratic nature of this form of local Government, the county or municipal council is structured to allow equal representation for all citizens within the area demarcated by the county boundaries or city limits. No one group of citizens is awarded greater representation than any other, regardless of their wealth or relative or equivalent "power" (whatever "power" means in a democratic paradigm).

County and City councils typically deal with issues and decisions that [b]*ONLY*[/b] affect the individual citizens within their democratically defined, geographic sphere. Issues that have (or would have) an impact on other citizens outside of the city or county, are dealt with at the next highest tier of democratic Government in the U.S: the State.

State Government within the U.S. are structured in accordance with State Law, including State Constitutions and State Statutes. Most State Governments are modelled on the Federal System, with three branches of Government: Executive; Legislative; and Judicial.

[quote][color=blue]Under the Tenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, all governmental powers not granted to the Federal Government by the U.S. Constitution, are reserved for the States or to the People.

The governments of the 13 colonies which formed the original union under the Constitution, trace their History back to the Royal Charters which established them during the years of colonialism. Most other states were organised as federal territories before forming their governments and requesting admittance into the Union.

Notable exceptions are California, Vermont, Texas and Hawaii, [b]which were sovereign nations before joining the Union[/b].[/color][/quote]

All U.S. State Governments are democratic, with officials elected by the Citizens of each State, with a mandate to govern and manage affairs and issues that are exclusive to that State. As members of a Union of States, individual States' Governments will [b]*ALSO*[/b] pass comment and resolutions on the conduct of the Federal Government; and in some cases - particularly Vermont and Texas - State Government will occasionally raise the prospect of secession from the Union, either to extract a particular advantage from the Federal Government (Texas), or to express its Citizens' disapproval at the course set for the Union by Federal Government's policies (Vermont).

The Federal Government's Legislative Branch - Congress - is a bicameral institution, consisting of the House of Representatives and the Senate.

The House of Representatives has 435 members, each of which represents a Congressional District and serves a two-year term. House seats are apportioned among the States by population.

The 100 Senators in the Senate, serve staggered six-year terms. Each State has two Senators, regardless of population.

Senators and Congressional Representatives democratically represent the Citizens of the United States; and the theory is that every Senator and Representative will promote the interests of the Citizens of their State or Congressional District.

A further theory is that no State or Congressional District has any greater power or influence in the Senate or House, than any other. Therefore, all Citizens of the Union are represented equally. In practice however, since every State has two Senators, regardless of population size, Citizens within the smaller States of the Union have a higher rate of [i]per capita[/i] emancipation in the Senate. Furthermore, certain Congressional Districts represent more wealthy, influential and powerful areas of the Union than others, and so certain seats in the House of Representatives are more highly prized than others.

The Citizens of the United States also directly elect the President of the Federal Government to the Executive Branch of the Government of the United States.

At all levels of Government, Citizens of the United States enjoy democratic representation by individual representatives who, in theory, promote the interests of those Citizens they represent.

At each level of Government, those representatives are concerned with issues and decisions that influence or impact the lives of those People these democratically representative positions exist to serve.

When the United Nations was conceived, [b]*BY THE LEADERS OF THE U.S. AND THE U.K.*[/b], at the end of World War Two, it was envisaged by the leaders of the Allied Nations (of World War Two), as a successor to the League of Nations.

Just as individual States in the U.S. and Parliamentary Districts in the U.K. are represented in democratic assemblies; it was intended that the United Nations would be a General Assembly of all the Nations of the World, where each Nation - regardless of its own internal democratic or non-democratic credentials - would possess a single vote.

This vote would [b]*NOT*[/b] be used to pass laws over other Nations, since the United Nations General Assembly and Security Council are [b]*NOT*[/b] legislative bodies. Rather the General Assembly and Security Council were assemblies that could and would pass [b]*RESOLUTIONS*[/b].

United Nations Resolutions are the diplomatic precursors to intra- and international agreements and treaties.

United Nations Resolutions, whether formed in the General Assembly or Security Council are [b]*NOT*[/b] legally binding articles of International Legislation.

The only aspect of the United Nations that has any domestic legal ramifications is the United Nations Charter, which is a diplomatic treaty that only has the force of domestic law, [b]*IF*[/b] - as in the case of the United States - the individual signatory nations' Constitution or laws award international treaties the same (or higher) status as domestic legislation.

The United Nations is [b]*NOT*[/b] a legislative or judicial body; nor does it have any executive function. The U.N. Secretary-General is just that, a Secretary, to the General Assembly: a civil servant; a mandarin.
