Well, the "Consensus" I mention amongst Economists is that the Global Economy [b]*CAN*[/b] be managed; not neccessarily [b]*HOW*[/b] it should be managed.

There are, broadly, two schools of thought in relation to Global Economics:

[link=http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keynesian_economics]Keynesian Economics[/link]

...Which is based on the work of the 20th Century Economist, John Maynard Keynes. [b]*VERY*[/b] loosely and broadly, Keynesian Economics promotes the idea of a form of regulated Capitalism, where the State stimulates Private Sector financial activity, through interest rates, taxation and public works.

This seems to be the fall-back position of most modern Democracies, but often carries too much of a whiff of Socialism to be widely accepted in, uh, certain Societies.

And then there is what is loosely referred to as [link=http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago_school_of_economics]Neo-Classical Economics[/link], which is most fully epitomised by the monetarist ideology of the Chicago School of Economics.

The name you are probably most familiar with, in connection with the Chicago School of Economics, is [link=http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman]Milton Friedman[/link].

During the 1980's, both Ronald Reagan and Margaret Thatcher were siezed by the grip of Friedman-ite Economic Theory, and set about transferring any and all formerly nationalised industries into private hands.

CSE/Friedman-ite/Monetarist Economic Theory tends to focus exclusively on the supply of money within an Economy, and its relation to inflation. Friedman posited that a balanced relationship between the supply of and demand for money, would produce an Economic equilibrium that would deliver greater social stability.

A prerequisite for the implementation of Friedman's theories was that the State move out of the business of, uh, Business... And concentrate solely on managing the supply of money.

This principle was/is coupled with the concept of the "Free Market", to justify privatising essential utilities, with the argument that a desire for profit would ensure that privately run utilities would be more efficient than Nationalised - and therefore bureaucratised - utility operation.

The end result of 3 decades of Friedman-ite/CSE/Monetarist Economic Theory, is that the former bastions of the Private Economy - the Banks - have now all but been nationalised...

Which pretty much shows that the whole concept of a Monetarist Economic Policy was and is complete bollocks.

And so the Governments in the Developed World are now retreating to that fallback Keynesian position I mentioned earlier.

There are other schools of thought, of course, and one of the most credible is that represented by Nobel Economics Laureate, [link=http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Stiglitz]Joseph Stiglitz[/quote], who as a more conservative economist, has been very critical of what he calls "Free Market Fundamentalists.

As for a compiled source for an Economic consensus 'Slacks, I'm not really aware of any "Popular Guides to Economics" that are of any use, either in print or on the Web.

Whilst some say that Economics is as much an Art as it is a Science, most Economists prefer to think of themselves as pure Social Scientists. However, as with the Sciences that contribute to Climate Change Theory, they are an ornery lot, and not renowned for being disposed towards consensus.

That said, there are a couple of organisations that I pay attention to.

One is the group of which Joseph Stiglitz is a co-founder, [link=http://www0.gsb.columbia.edu/ipd/]the Initiative for Policy Dialogue[/link], based at Columbia University (where Stiglitz is an Economics professor.

The other is a British group called the [link=http://www.neweconomics.org/gen/]New Economics Foundation[/link].

Both are reasonable places to start if you want to get a feel for what Economists are thinking.

Whilst Economists don't generally agree on a single theory or method for managing the Global Economy; they do all tend to agree that it can and should be managed.

The only people who disagreed with that principle, the so-called "Free Market Fundamentalists", were more Financiers than they were Economists; more interested in self-enrichment away from the prying eyes of public scrutiny, than creating a workable and sustainable model for Global Economics.
