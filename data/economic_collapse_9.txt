[color=blue][b]Meltdown Looms Larger as Credit Markets Freeze

[i]By MIKE WHITNEY [/i][/b]

[i]"It's another round of the credit crisis. Some markets are getting worse than January this time. There is fear that something dramatic will happen and that fear is feeding itself,"[/i] - Jesper Fischer-Nielsen, interest rate strategist at Danske Bank, Copenhagen; Reuters

[color=blue]In his prepared statement, Ben Bernanke (Chairman of the Federal Reserve) announced that the Fed would add $200 billion to the financial system to shore up banks that have been battered by mortgage-related losses. The news was greeted with jubilation on Wall Street where traders sent stocks skyrocketing by 416 points, their biggest one-day gain in five years.

[i]"It's like they're putting jumper cables onto a battery to kick-start the credit market,'' said Nick Raich, a manager at National City Private Client Group in Cleveland. ``They're doing their best to try to restore confidence.''[/i]

To understand the real meaning behind the Fed's action; it's worth considering some of the stories which popped up in the business news just days earlier. For example, last Friday, the International Herald Tribune reported:

[i]"Tight money markets, tumbling stocks and the dollar are expected to heighten worries for investors this week as pressure mounts on central banks facing what looks like the "third wave" of a global credit crisis....Money markets tightened to levels not seen since December, when year-end funding problems pushed lending costs higher across the board."[/i]

The Herald Tribune said that troubles in the credit markets had pushed the stock market down more than 3 percent in a week and that the same conditions which preceded the last two crises (in August and December) were back stronger than ever. In other words, liquidity was vanishing from the system and the market was headed for a crash.[/color]

This refers to the so-called "Credit Crunch", where as a result of profligate, indiscriminate and unregulated lending practices, banks and other financial institutions have loaned out far more money than they had to back up those loans.

As a result, these same institutions have now all but frozen their lending, meaning that there is little or no more credit available to anyone.

[color=blue]A report in Reuters reiterated the same ominous prediction of a "third wave" saying:

[i]"The two-year U.S. Treasury yields hit a 4-year low below 1.5 percent as investors flocked to safe-haven government bonds....The cost of corporate bond insurance hit record highs on Friday and parts of the debt market which had previously escaped the turmoil are also getting hit."[/i][/color]

Corporate Bond Insurance is the insurance that underwrites large-scale loans. Basically like your "Payment Protection Insurance" on your credit-cards and personal loans.

It's one of the next big shocks heading towards the system that basically no-one is willing to underwrite any more loans. Without loan insurance, it becomes even more difficult to arrange credit terms.

[color=blue]Risk premiums were soaring and investors were fleeing stocks and bonds for the safety of government Treasuries; another sure sign that liquidity was disappearing.

[i]Reuters: "The level of financial stress is ... likely to continue to fuel speculation of more immediate central bank action either in the form of increased liquidity injections or an early rate cut," Goldman Sachs said in a note to clients."[/i]

Indeed. When there's a funding-freeze by lenders, investors hit the exits as fast as their feet will carry them. That's why the lights started blinking red at the Federal Reserve and Bernanke concocted a plan to add $200 billion to the listing banking system.

New York Times columnist Paul Krugman also referred to a "third wave" in his article "The Face-Slap Theory". According to Krugman, [i]"The Fed has been cutting the interest rate it controls - the so-called Fed funds rate - (but) the rates that matter most directly to the economy, including rates on mortgages and corporate bonds, have been rising. And that's sure to worsen the economic downturn."...(Now) "the banks and other market players who took on too much risk are all trying to get out of unsafe investments at the same time, causing significant collateral damage to market functioning." What the Times' columnist is describing is a run on the financial system and the onset of "a full-fledged financial panic."[/i][/color]

So even though the Federal Reserve has lowered the rate at which it is willing to lend money to financial institutions, those institutions are not passing on those cuts to their customers, and in many cases are still increasing their interest rates.

[color=blue]The point is, Bernanke's latest scheme is not a remedy for the trillion dollar unwinding of bad bets. It is merely a quick-fix to avoid a bloody stock market crash brought on by prevailing conditions in the credit markets.

Bernanke coordinated the action with the other members of the global banking cartel---The Bank of Canada, the Bank of England, the European Central Bank, the Federal Reserve, and the Swiss National Bank---and cobbled together the new Term Securities Lending Facility (TSLF), which [i]"will lend up to $200 billion of Treasury securities to primary dealers secured for a term of 28 days (rather than overnight, as in the existing program) by a pledge of other securities, including federal agency debt, federal agency residential-mortgage-backed securities (MBS), and non-agency AAA/Aaa-rated private-label residential MBS. The TSLF is intended to promote liquidity in the financing markets for Treasury and other collateral and thus to foster the functioning of financial markets more generally." (Fed statement)[/i][/color]

In conjunction with other Central (Government-ish) Banks, the Federal Reserve has concocted a new scheme to lend money (up to $200 billion) to financial institutions on much more favourable terms than normal.

[color=blue]The plan, of course, is wildly inflationary and will put additional downward pressure on the anemic dollar. No matter. All of the Fed's tools are implicitly inflationary anyway, but they'll all be put to use before the current crisis is over.[/color]

The problem with the Fed's plan is that it will put inflationary pressure on the U.S. Dollar, meaning it will become worth less and less.

[color=blue]The Fed's statement continues: [i]"The Federal Open Market Committee has authorized increases in its existing temporary reciprocal currency arrangements (swap lines) with the European Central Bank (ECB) and the Swiss National Bank (SNB). These arrangements will now provide dollars in amounts of up to $30 billion and $6 billion to the ECB and the SNB, respectively, representing increases of $10 billion and $2 billion. The FOMC extended the term of these swap lines through September 30, 2008."[/i]

So, why is the Fed issuing loans to foreign banks? Isn't that a tacit admission of its guilt in the trillion dollar subprime swindle? In any event, the Fed's largess proves that the G-10 operates as de facto cartel determining monetary policy for much of the world. (The G-10 represents roughly 85% of global GDP)[/color]

Whitney is speculating here on why the U.S. Federal Reserve has come up with such favourable terms to lend (or give away...) money to [b]*FOREIGN*[/b] banks.

He speculates that because the current financial crisis is by and large the result of U.S. monetary and fiscal policy over the last six or seven years, the new loan terms of the TSLF are a way of sweetening foreign investors, hopefully to prevent them turning against the U.S. Economy, legally and economically.

[color=blue]As for Bernanke's Term Securities Lending Facility (TSLF) it is intentionally designed to circumvent the Fed's mandate to only take top-grade collateral in exchange for loans. No one believes that these triple A mortgage-backed securities are worth more than $.70 on the dollar. In fact, according to a report in Bloomberg News yesterday: [i]"AAA debt fell as low as 61 cents on the dollar after record home foreclosures and a decline to AA may push the value of the debt to 26 cents, according to Credit Suisse Group.`The fact that they've kept those ratings where they are is laughable,'' said Kyle Bass, chief executive officer of Hayman Capital Partners, a Dallas-based hedge fund that made $500 million last year betting lower-rated subprime-mortgage bonds would decline in value. ``Downgrades of AAA and AA bonds are imminent, and they're going to be significant.'' Another gift from Santa Bernanke.[/color]

The U.S. Federal Reserve is a private banking institution (unlike the Bank of England or the European Central Bank), and has long had an uneasy relationship with the U.S. Government and Department of the Treasury. Several past Presidents - including Lincoln and Kennedy - have looked to abolish the Federal Reserve and replace it with a more usual Government Central Bank.

The Fed tries to walk a fine line between doing what is best for the U.S. Economy and tax-payers, and what profits the (mostly European) owners of the Federal Reserve.

Part of the arrangement is that the Federal Reserve is only permitted to take the highest grades of collateral against the loans it gives out. High grade collateral is things like the ownership of a functioning and performing bank, and other businesses and properties rated "AAA".

The problem is that because of the current turmoil, there's actually very little left in the Global Economy that can reliably be considered "AAA". This means that the Federal Reserve, under normal circumstances, [b]*CAN'T*[/b] lend money to anyone, to try to avert the impending crisis.

So the terms by which Bernanke has set-up the TSLF is an attempt to circumvent this restriction.

[color=blue]Additionally, the Fed is offering 28 day repos which -- if this auction works like the Fed's other facility, the TAF -- the loans can be rolled over free of charge for another 28 days. Yippee. The Fed found a way to recapitalize the banks with permanent rotating loans and the public is none the wiser. The capital-starved banksters at Citi and Merrill must feel like they just won the lottery. Unfortunately, Bernanke's move effectively nationalizes the banks and makes them entirely dependent on the Fed's fickle generosity.[/color]

This is an important point.

With the new arrangements announced by the Fed, the risks that private banks and financial institutions incur are now underwritten by the Federal Reserve and U.S. Government.

The best analogy to explain this is to imagine you are the owner of a casino that is losing customers.

To encourage customers back into your casino, you announce that any losses they incur will be covered by you. Anybody who bets in your casino, whilst not guaranteed to win, [b]*IS*[/b] guaranteed not to lose.

However, that there is still no guarantee of winning, and actually, losses will only be covered up to a point, may mean that these measures are still not enough.

The usual response of a Central Bank facing economic crises is to print more money.

Commercial and retail banks borrow money to lend it, either from depositors, or from a National Central Bank.

When a Central Bank needs money, it just prints more.

Problem with that, of course, is that the more printed money there is in circulation, the less it is worth.

And on top of that, there are limits to how much money a Central Bank can print, before it tips over into the kind of hyper-inflation most famously seen in Germany following World War One.
