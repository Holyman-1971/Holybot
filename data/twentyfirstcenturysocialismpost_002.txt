[color=#004000]Where's the motivation for someone to send up an enterprise or invest capital if they don't at least have much more of a say than the workers in the enterprise? And if we transitioned to such a model, wouldn't larger enterprises with large financial stakeholders simply take their business elsewhere?[/color][/quote]

The motivation to establish and grow an Enterprise is rarely about wanting to exert control or dominance over other human beings. And where that is the motivation, the Enterprise is generally unsuccessful.

A more common motivation is an attempt to create a new market for new products, or to create and market established products more effectively than others are doing.

We can use Jeff Bezos (founder of Amazon) as a good example, mostly because he has testified at length about his journey. Though he has become one of the wealthiest individuals in the human world, that was never his intention or motivation. He was motivated to deliver one of the oldest trades (selling books) more effectively than established providers. He realised that emerging technologies provided the opportunity to improve things for book buyers: for example, offering them a wider range of books to choose from than they could find in any physical store. So he set about doing that, and things really took off for him.

Crucial to Amazon's incredible success over the last two decades has been the fact that Jeff Bezos declares himself to be, [url=https://www.businessinsider.com/jeff-bezos-pictures-amazon-as-inverted-2018-11?r=US&IR=T]"the least important person at Amazon"[/url].

From the outset, Bezos deferred to the individuals who joined with his enterprise and mandated that they too must always defer to the individuals they engaged. He understood that those who were actually delivering Amazon's services and engaging with clients, would have a better grasp on what those clients were looking for, than senior executives who were isolated from the client base.

When it came to individuals and teams within the Amazon enterprise exploring and developing new initiatives, the ethos was one of encouragement and a high degree of autonomy. The result of that ethos is that you are as likely to be buying IT infrastructure-as-a-service from Amazon these days, as you are to be buying a book.

These days [url=https://www.investopedia.com/articles/insights/052816/top-4-amazon-shareholders-amzn.asp]Jeff Bezos only owns around 10% of Amazon's stock[/url], though he is still the largest single shareholder. Given that Amazon's current market capitalisation is around $1.4trillion, that isn't too bad a stake!

[url=https://companiesmarketcap.com/]Amazon is the fifth largest corporation in the world[/url], by Market Cap, below Alphabet(/Google) in fourth place, Saudi Aramco in third, Microsoft in second, and Apple in first. The staggering rise of four out of five of those corporations have been based primarily on decentralised decision making and the encouragement of individuals' creativity and innovation.

When I got my first gig at a bank (@Goldman Sachs in 1995), Investment Bankers were the "[url=https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/politicsandpolicy/masters-of-the-universe-slaves-of-the-market/]Masters of the Universe[/url]". Banking organisations are built on structures and cultures that are around 500 years old, and they have not adapted to new Realities.

Paleo-Banks still work on the lines you've described Slacks, where the senior executives and shareholders get to dictate what happens within their highly-complex organisations, without in most cases really understanding anything at all about what is going on any more. That is why we now have [url=https://thefintechtimes.com/what-is-a-challenger-bank/]"Challenger Banks"[/url] operating much more successfully than their palaeolithic competitors.

The key then, to success and survival in this Modern World, is to operate organisations and enterprises as holistic, non-hierarchical, dynamic systems, with no single individual more important (or more listened to) than any other. If you receive a salary or wage from an enterprise, then you are necessary for that enterprise to function, whether you are the CEO or the person who keeps the toilets hygienic and usable.

It may be the case that it is easier to find alternative human individuals to fulfil certain functions than others, and this will be reflected in the remuneration level for each function. However, the idea that it is possible for one individual (or a small group of individuals) to understand the complexities of organisational operations and priorities, is no longer realistic.

It is as it has always been: the Whole needs to function effectively. And deference to any single individual is no longer a formula for success.
