The issue with stimulus programmes isn't political though, it is Economic.

When the Private Sector contracts, unemployment rises and wages are depressed.

It then becomes a vicious cycle: as more people lose their jobs, or their pay rises, or take pay cuts, there is less disposable income flowing through the Economy. And this means the Private Sector contracts even further, more people lose their jobs, and wages fall even further.

And on top of all that, tax revenues also fall because fewer people are paying income tax and fewer corporations are paying less tax on fewer profits.

How do you break this downward spiral?

The only solution that presents itself (and I'm not saying it is the best or perfect solution - just that is the only one I've heard of that sounds logical), is for a government to borrow money to invest in stimulus programmes.

Creating jobs and contracts within the Public Sector is a method of injecting money into the Economy to stimulate the "heartbeat" of the Private Sector. Borrowed money spent on stimulus programmes, decreases unemployment and raises wages. The increase in disposable income is then directed to the Private Sector, which having become leaner and more efficient during the recession, is able to get its "heartbeat" going again.

As the Private Sector moves into the upward phase of the Capitalist Economic Cycle, the vicious circle becomes virtuous. Unemployment decreases further, wages rise, more money is spent on Private Sector goods and services, and the Private Sector expands.

Furthermore, as the Private Sector expands, more people pay income tax and more companies pay more tax on more profits. The increased tax revenue is then used to pay back the borrowing used to fund the stimulus programmes, and Public Sector activity and employment is scaled back.

Now as I'm sure you know AV, the above is pure Keynesianism, named for John Maynard Keynes, the guy who first elucidated that theory in the 1930's.

What you may not have appreciated is that Keynes was entirely a-political: he didn't have any ideological axe to grind - or if he did, it isn't evident in the theory that bears his name.

It is important to understand that Keynes' theory was an articulation of the only response possible to the contraction of the Private Sector, within the Capitalist Economic Model. It was certainly *NOT* an attempt at "Backdoor Socialism".

Indeed, Keynes felt strongly that what most threatened the fluidity and simplicity of his Theory, was that Governments would *NOT* scale back their stimulus programmes, once they had achieved their objectives.

In many ways, Keynes was a High Priest of Capitalism, in that he came up with about the only sound theory there is, which can manage a response to the cyclical nature of Economic Capitalism. Without Keynes' defined cyclical approach, it is doubtful that Capitalism would have survived the increasingly intense highs and lows of the past hundred years.

Bringing things up-to-date, we can see by the responses to the failure of the Banking System in 2008, that those governments - such as the United States and Sweden - that utilised the Keynesian response, are now showing healthy and positive signs of Private Sector recovery.

Whereas those governments that took the, shall we say, Chicago School of Economics' "Efficient Market" approach - such as Ireland, Spain and the United Kingdom - are either still mired in recession, or are completely bankrupt.

Set aside political ideology and your own morality for a moment AV. Can you really think of a response to the Economic contraction of the Private Sector that is demonstrably more pragmatic and effective than Keynesianism?

For one thing there IS an issue of misallocation of resources, if infrastructure was totally privatized, then those greedy business men working under the influences of the profit motive and rational self interest, would always, to the best of their ability, build projects where there is the greatest profit, that is, the greatest demand. I think the best approach is to view the Government as the primary client and stakeholder, not the owner or manager of infrastructure and utilities.

Politicians and Civil Servants should not be in the business of managing engineering and maintenance operations. However, in that Government represents the collective purchasing power of the primary users of infrastructure and utilities, it is - if it feels so inclined - able to set higher performance measures for those companies that [i]are[/i] in the business of managing engineering and maintenance operations.

Performance measures for infrastructure, utilities, and other basic social necessities are really easy to set: Everything Must Work, Safely.

There is little "Value Add" in the provision of roads and say, water supply. They just have to work safely and the end user is satisfied.

I think it is not only appropriate but actually a [i]requirement[/i] that contracts for construction and maintenance of infrastructure and utilities are awarded to Private Sector operations. The Private Sector is best equipped to focus on their particular fields of expertise, and to direct funding at research and innovation more effectively than a single, central government can.

The awarding of those contracts must, of course, be carried out in a fair and completely transparent manner. And those organisations that are awarded the contracts must be held accountable to mutually agreed performance measures.

Currently, such franchises seem to be more about reward, than award.

That is, reward for financial contributions and "donations", in the form of lengthy franchises, determined by opaque procurement deals, with performance metrics dictated by the supplier rather than the client.

On the other hand, I do not feel that it is appropriate for government to award/reward contracts for the ownership of infrastructure and utility [b]*OPERATION*[/b].

The "drive to [b]*IMPROVE*[/b] one's own condition" *IS* a key motivator of human action.

However, the [b]*NEED TO SURVIVE*[/b] is a vital prerequisite for human action, motivation and improvement.

Everyone needs water. Not everyone needs a bigger house.

Everyone needs energy to cook and heat. Not everyone needs a Ferrari.

Everyone needs to be able to travel to work, to school, to shop and to go see their families. Not everyone needs to do that faster and in more luxury than everyone else.

Everyone needs a basic level of sustenance and nutrition. Not everyone needs to eat caviar and drink champagne.

The drive and motivation to improve one's own condition is and should be a matter of personal responsibility and ambition.

That we would seek to ensure that we all have the basic necessities for survival should be a matter of collective responsibility and duty.
