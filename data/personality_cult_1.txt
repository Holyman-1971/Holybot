w��������As far as I am aware, Paul Craig Roberts (PCR) is considered to be (and I believe considers his self to be) a Paleoconservative (Paleo-Conservative?).

It's a relatively new term that has been coined as a direct result of the rise of the Neo-Conservatives in Washington particularly, and across America in general.

Here's what Wikipedia has to say about Paleoconservatism:

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paleoconservatism

In short then, PCR is an "old school" Republican, a card-carrying member of the Grand Ole Party, rather than an acolyte of Leo Strauss. He believes in small government, is widely and vocally opposed to immigration, possesses the kind of isolationist tendencies that characterised the United States' attitude to foreign policy right up to Pearl Harbour, and favours de-centralised Federalism over centralised Nationalism.

The majority of PCR's writings concern the state of the United States' economy, and his particular b�te noire is the transfer of America's middle-class/tradable jobs (particularly in high-tech industries), out of the States, to India and other places. This is something he believes will lead to the ruin of the United States' Economy, which has seen job growth only in domestic/non-tradable jobs such as cleaning and food service, since the end of the Dot-Com boom.

But you know, all this highlights an interesting and not entirely healthy aspect of the nature of political debate, especially in the United States, but more generally in all the World's "Democracies". And that is that the method and manner of popular expression is now all about personalities and partisanship, at the expense of ideological substance.

For instance, in the two PCR articles in this thread, and in the speech referenced and given by Al Gore, many important issues and ideas have been raised, but these are all ignored in the resulting discourse, in favour of attacks, defence, critiques and assessments of the personalities expressing their opinions.

The idea seems to be, when faced with a rational argument, to ignore the intellectual and semantic composition and expression of opinion, and aim straight for an assassination of character and motive.

Now it isn't that ad hominem attacks are anti-social, immoral or offensive, it's just that they are stupid, lazy and pointless. Worse than that, they distract us from the actually important business of discussing how our lives are governed, and turns politics into a personality-based popularity contest.

Take for example this excerpt from the first PCR piece:

In this first decade of the 21st century the United States regards itself as a land of democracy and civil liberty but, in fact, is an incipient dictatorship. Ideology plays only a limited role in the emerging dictatorship. The demise of American democracy is largely the result of historical developments.

Lincoln was the first American tyrant. Lincoln justified his tyranny in the name of preserving the Union. His extra-legal, extra-constitutional methods were tolerated in order to suppress Northern opposition to Lincoln's war against the Southern secession.

The idea expressed in the first paragraph is not that Bush and/or the Neo-Conservatives are engaged in a concerted and determined effort to turn the American Republic into a Dictatorship; but rather that historical developments and trends in the relationship between the governors and the governed have created the conditions where the slide into dictatorship is an inevitable, if unintended consequence.

In the second paragraph, PCR opens with the strong phrase: "Lincoln was the first American tyrant." As a previous poster stated, he stopped reading when he came to this statement. The thing is, PCR isn't passing a subjective judgement on someone who has been dead for nearly a century and a half, but is using the term "tyrant" in its literal, objective sense, referring to someone who placed his self, and his decisions, above the Law.

The fact that Lincoln *did* suspend several aspects of the American Constitution is a matter of historical record. Those actions however are now generally considered to be an acceptable necessity, but actually, only because the North won the Civil War. Had the Southern Secessionists triumphed in the American Civil War, it is doubtless that Lincoln's (temporary?) suspension of parts of the Constitution and (necessary?) use of tyrannical authority, would have been used to demonise the leader of the losing side.

That's debatable and unknowable, but the fact remains that Lincoln did assume (strictly speaking) tyrannical powers for a short period, but then surrendered them once the time of turbulence had passed. However, because PCR dared to use the word "tyrant" in relation to the Sacred Cow that is President Lincoln (declared by Gore in his speech to be the "Greatest President"), the substantive content of his article can be dismissed in a flurry of sanctimonious offence-seeking.

The general point in the first PCR article is that whilst the suspension of the Constitution and wider civil liberties is not unprecedented in American History, it has only ever been a temporary measure. In the context of the declared "War on Terror", a "War" we are told that may have no end, there is no fixed end-point for these suspensions.

Surely if we are to support and conform to such suspensions, we ought to demand a clear-cut date or set of conditions that would see the most precious articles of the Constitution restored.

It is somewhat ironic that the pro-Gun (and largely Republican) lobby uses a flexible and often disingenuous claim to rights enshrined in the Constitution to maintain their right to be personally, heavily armed; but when ordered to support the suspension of habeus corpus - a fundamental tenet of civilised law stretching back to Magna Carta � they will happily accept it as a necessity with no fixed date or condition for its restoration.

In the second article, PCR clearly demonstrates the supine and compromised position of the mainstream media, those so-called valiant seekers after truth. The two points (incidentally confused by the first person to respond) he uses to support this argument are that the New York Times sat on the FISA/Eavesdropping story for a year (as presumably ordered to by its proprietor), and failed to report at all the speech by Al Gore.
