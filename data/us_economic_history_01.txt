Prior to 1776 then, there was no such place as the United States, and no American Society, so we can discount the Economic History of England's North American colonies, most of which revolved around cotton, molasses and tobacco.

It is however worth pointing out that the American Revolution was not primarily catalysed by the Pursuit of Liberty for All Equal Men, but by a Middle-/Mercantile-Class desire to retain more profit (sound familiar?).

Up to 1776, the thirteen English colonies on the Eastern Seaboard of North America were used by the English Government purely as a cash-cow to fund its Global Imperial expansion, and to pay for its wars with its European rivals.

English colonial merchants in North America developed the idea that since they were so geographically distant from England, and England was perpetually fighting on its Eastern Front with the French, Dutch and Spanish, fomenting a rebellion that would result in their being able to retain a greater portion of the profit from their activities, was worth a shot.

The "Spin" taught (to this day) in U.S. Schools - and still the basic Operational Ideology of the United States of America, inc. - is that those who did all the hard work, should get to keep the bulk of the rewards. Why should "hard-working" merchants in the North American colonies have to handover so much of their profits in Excise Duty and Trade Tariffs, to fund England's martial activities in Europe?

But of course, this whole conceit falls apart on the basis of a single word: Slavery.

The "hardest" work any North American plantation owner ever had to engage in, was whipping slaves. But even there, they could usually find less-propertied white men who would do that for them, for a wage.

If the "Philosophy" was "Those who do the work, should keep the rewards", then all the slaves toiling in North America should have been as rich as Croesus...

Anyhoo, the Gamble of those merchants paid off: Westminster wasn't able to fight the French in Europe, India *AND* North America. Giving up the fight in Europe wasn't an option, so it was a choice between retaining either India or North America: and the English opted to go with the Sub-Continent, rather than the New World; retaining, of course, the consolation prize of Canada.

And just as the Founding Fathers were completing their Political Revolution; the Europeans in the Old War were embarking on an altogether more transformative Revolution: an Industrial one.

But whilst Europe industrialised, the new American Republic's Economy remained squarely agricultural: crops and furs in the middle and northern colonies, and slavery plantations in the South. In 1789, 90% of U.S. citizens were employed, and 99% of U.S. slaves enslaved, in agricultural activities.

It was an Englishman, [url=https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Slater]Samuel Slater[/url], who is regarded (in the U.S.) as the "Father of the American Industrial Revolution" (in the UK, he was called "Slater the Traitor"), who arrived in the U.S. with memorised designs of Jebediah Strutt's Derbyshire cotton mill.

[url=https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eli_Whitney]Eli Whitney[/url] then invented the [url=https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_gin]Cotton Gin[/url], and for the first half of the 19th Century, the U.S. Economy was firmly centred in the South. By 1850, cotton production in the U.S. had expanded to 2.85 million bales a year: a four-fold increase on the output in 1830.

Correspondingly, the number of slaves engaged in cotton production expanded from around 700,000 in 1790, to 3.2 million in 1850. And since slavery was by that point illegal everywhere in Europe, the U.S. arrived at its first position of significant Global Trade advantage.

And then gold was discovered in California, in 1848.

This dragged a large number of Americans "Out West", and it also shifted the balance of Economic power in the U.S. from the South to the North.

And consequently, there was a Civil War.

Which economically destroyed the Southern United States.

Since slavery was no longer (strictly) legal after the Civil War, [url=https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton_gin]"Sharecropping"[/url] became the foundation of the economy of the Southern U.S. Sharecropping was basically the same form of agricultural production used in Europe between the Dark Ages (c. 600 C.E.) and the Renaissance (c. 1500 C.E.).

But in the Northern States, Industrial Expansion was the order of the day.

In 1870, the U.S. share of global GDP was 9% (UK, France & Germany combined was 22%; China was 17%; and India 12%).

By 1913, it was at 19%: just behind the UK, France & Germany combined, still at 22%; but now ahead of India & China combined at 18%.

Of course, it always pays to read the small-print... And inserted into the T's & C's - and British desperation for funds made it non-negotiable - was a condition requiring the convertibility of the British Pound Sterling. One year after the loan was ratified, nations with Sterling balances were now permitted to sell those Pounds to the UK in exchange for Dollars. And since the Dollar was just about the only Hard Currency left in the post-World War Two World, this led to immediate drastic cuts in British domestic and overseas expenditure: i.e. "Austerity" at Home; and complete dismantlement of Britain's Empire.

The UK did miss the dates for a few of those payments, but eventually settled up with a final payment of $83million on the 29th December, 2006.
