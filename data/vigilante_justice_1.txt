I think the conflict in the debate is between the specific and the general, the micro- and the macro-, the incident and the policy.

Taking the specific incident, it's kind of difficult [b]*NOT*[/b] to understand the predicament of the Texan, Mr. Horn.

It's not entirely clear from this one report, because it contains contradictory statements:

From Mr. Horn's attorney:

[quote][i][color=red]"He was afraid for his life," Lambright said. "He was afraid for his safety, [b]and then they charged him.[/b] I don't think Joe had time to make a conscious decision. I think he only had time to react to what was going on. Short answer is, he was defending his life." [/color][/i][/quote]

But from the attending Police Officer:

[quote][i][color=blue]"Both suspects were shot in the back," Pasadena Police Captain A.H. "Bud" Corbett said. "Not at the same angle, but both suspects were hit in the back."[/color][/i][/quote]

But if we throw in the dialogue with the dispatcher:

[quote][i][color=green]"I'm gonna kill him," Horn said.

"Stay in the house," the dispatcher said.

"They're getting away," Horn replied.

"That's all right," the dispatcher said. "Property's not worth killing someone over. OK?"

"---damn it," said Horn, who then defied the dispatcher.[/color][/i][/quote]

Then it starts to look like this wasn't an act of necessary self-defence, but rather a pre-meditated execution by a vigilante.

[b]"I'm gonna kill him"[/b] and [b]"They're getting away"[/b], says Mr. Horn.

One way or another, he ends up confronting them, but after that, we don't know if they did charge at him, because Mr. Horn is the only witness to the incident still alive. The fact that they were shot in their backs isn't conclusive, because we don't know if they turned around at the last minute, when they fully understood Mr. Horn's serious intent.

What we can fairly surmise is that Mr. Horn's blood was up. His adrenalin was pumping and he was acting on instinct and emotion. He's armed; he's protecting his neighbour's property; and it appears that the Grand Jury found that he acted legally.

And let's face it, in the deaths of the two burglars, it is unlikely that Humanity has lost a cancer cure or anything similar.

But now we've got to roll it up to the general policy, the Law of the Land.

Vigilante "justice" doesn't work.

The scope for miscarriages of justice, over-reactions, and worse crimes being committed in the punishment of lesser crimes is simply too great.

It is why we, in the Civilised World, don't rely on vigilante justice.

Everyone is human, but trained Law Enforcement Officials do have at least a marginally better control of their emotions in such situations, and a greater knowledge of the Law and an understanding of proportionality: Mr. Horn when straight to a capital punishment for burglary.Due process carried out by trained Law Enforcement Officials minimises (though it doesn't completely eliminate) miscarriages of justice. It might not be a perfect solution, but we know that it is a better solution than vigilante "justice", which is easily abused and hi-jacked to settle personal scores.

So the policy must be that law enforcement remains the responsibility and duty of trained officials; but each case where a citizen takes the law into their own hands, must be judged on the individual circumstance.

I think Mr. Horn probably did the only thing he was capable of doing at the time. Only he really knows whether or not it was absolutely necessary for him to take those two human lives; and that will be something his conscience will wrestle with for the rest of his days.
