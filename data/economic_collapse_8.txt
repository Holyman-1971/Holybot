To give you a little background about me Border: I spent the better part of the 1990's working for investment and merchant banks in the City of London. I was a freelance IT consultant, and worked with banks such as Goldman Sachs, Citibank, Royal Bank of Scotland and others, designing and developing dealer support systems.

My work gradually evolved into strategic information and business intelligence management, and I now work closely with the UK Central Government across most departments, various financial institutions, including the Bank of England, and many high-profile private sector operations.

My current activities then can be broadly defined as: knowing "What's Going On"; and ensuring those who [b]*NEED*[/b] to know "What's Going On", know What Is Going On.

Now I wouldn't last very long in my current role, if I took it upon myself to decide for those others, what [b]*I*[/b] think [b]*THEY*[/b] should think, Is Going On. To this end, it is imperative that the information I provide, and help others to source themselves, is as pure as possible, and unpolluted by bias and subjectivity as is feasible.

One of the greatest challenges for governmental and business leaders these days is sifting through the overwhelming amounts of information that are available to them, to get to the intelligence they really need to do their jobs.

Reverse engineering the "Spin" put on a lot of information, is at least as much of an art, as spinning the information in the first place. There's a fairly straightforward equation used in message delivery these days:

Information + Spin = Message

The "Message" is obviously easy to find and catalogue: we are all inundated with "Messages" every day of our lives. And the "Spin" is usually a straightforward matter to work out. And this leaves the resulting reversed equation as:

Message - Spin = Information

Augmented by a reliable intelligence (in all senses of the word), it becomes a fairly straight-forward process to divine and distil valid information, from the "Messages" and marketing that incessantly fill our airwaves, newsprint media and the Internet.

I say all this Border, in part to establish my credentials for what will follow; but also to create the context for a somewhat bold assertion. And that assertion is:

We are now living in a Post-Ideological World.

What I mean by that is not that we live in a World devoid of Idealism - I count myself as a passionate Idealist - but that the Day and Age (and relevance) of political ideology has past.

Human Society is moving at an increasingly higher frequency, exponentially accelerating as Time goes by. The idea that we still retain the luxury of having personal, ideological dispositions, through which we can or must view the World, is all but redundant.

Simply: the idea that we can make up our minds about an issue or situation, before we have learned all there is to learn about it, is looking increasingly absurd.

Whether our "Playbook" is "Reaganomics", "Das Kapital", or "Stock Investments for Dummies", what we're seeing now is that a fixed attitude or preconceived strategy for managing and guiding our interactions with the World - particularly the World of Finance - is more of a hindrance than a help.

Actually, that said, maybe there is one playbook that is still relevant, and that must be "The Law of Sod". Fortunately, it's quite a brief playbook, containing only a single entry:

"Sod's Law mandates that whatever you think must, should or is most likely to happen: it will almost always be the opposite of that, which [b]*ACTUALLY*[/b] happens."

So the conclusion I personally draw from this theorising, is that whatever else, it behoves us all to keep an Open Mind about Everything.

In this Quantum World of Information we now exist in, even the slightest, seemingly most inconsequential piece of information, can turn out to have been the critical or deciding factor in the resulting outcome.

Therefore, to wed ourselves to a particular ideology, or "playbook", is to risk closing our minds to certain information or ideas that could prove crucial to the successful outcome of our endeavours.

It isn't then a question of dropping your "C/conservative Playbook", and exchanging it for a "Liberal Playbook" (Neo- or otherwise); but rather dropping the whole concept of playbooks altogether, and relying instead on the freshest and most comprehensive flow of information you can manage.

In other words: Keeping Your Mind Open, to All Possibilities.

Only then, I think, do we permit ourselves to be agile enough to surf the rushing waves of fate before us. :)

I think you've hit the nail on the head.

The World has changed, and continues to change rapidly. Denying the scale and scope of that change, makes a once sensible financial strategy seem both parochial and anachronistic.

The only real assessment these days, can be based on the observation and measurement of continued effectiveness. Clearly, the growth of the Global Marketplace has seen a positive and creative reliance on comparative advantage, become a rapacious scramble for [b]*ABSOLUTE*[/b] advantage.

Here's an interesting article on the recent phenomenon of "Labour Arbitrage":

http://www.dailyreckoning.co.uk/article/060320063.html

The problem with maintaining a reliance on "Trickle-down" economics - or the less derogatory and more factual term, Supply-side Economics - is that it doesn't take account of the fact that Capital, in its Global (and final?) incarnation, no longer respects national boundaries.

In other words, it is no longer a given that what "Trickles Down" as a result of government policy, will actually trickle-down through the economy for which that government is actually responsible.

To look at the most obvious example: the tax cuts enacted by the Bush Administration early in its first term, were (probably... ;)) intended to increase and expand business activity within the borders of the United States. But coinciding as they did with a massive wave of American businesses off-shoring their principal activities to countries with lower wages and regulation, meant that actually, what trickled-down from those tax-cuts, actually trickled down to the Chinese, Indian and Latin American consumers, rather than consumers in the U.S., as was (probably...) intended.

Whoever else - outside of America's Business Elite - benefited from Bush's tax cuts, it most certainly was [b]*NOT*[/b] the American Middle or Working classes; who for the better part have seen their wages stagnate, and in a lot of cases, actually decline.

The improvements in lifestyle that (some) Americans have enjoyed since the beginning of the 21st Century have not been as a result of Bush's tax cuts; but the result of consumers borrowing on unconscionably easy lines of credit.

Credit that now has to be repaid.

One of the issues with fixing on a specific ideological strategy, is that it tends to make us think in binary alternatives. It is "This", [b]*OR*[/b] it is "That". It is Supply Side Economics, [b]*OR*[/b] it is Demand Side; Friedman or Keynes; pure Capitalism or pure Socialism.

But like you say Border: why [b]*CAN'T*[/b] Capitalism have some Socialism? And why can't Socialism have some Capitalism?

Why can't we take the best of each, and discard the worst?

It seems so sensible and obvious. But our politicians are wedded to their ideological identities - at least, the ideological identities of those who vote for them - and it doesn't leave a lot of wiggle-room for them: no politician wants to be accused of being a "flip-flopper".

To me, it seems obvious that there are certain services and supplies that should obviously be delivered socially; and the rest which should be delivered subject to the conditions of The Market.

At this point in the conversation, I usually ask the following question:

Is it morally or practically appropriate that one human being should profit from another human being's need for water?

Well, some respondents say that practically, if you want people to run the water treatment plants, and fix the pipes, you've got to offer them some incentive to do so. (Only the most morose and personally aggrieved try to argue that it might be morally appropriate...)

To which I say: if a person is motivated by personal, material profit, then maybe they should consider an alternative career to one in the Water Supply Industry.

There [b]*ARE*[/b] many, many people who are willing to devote their lives to the service of Society, without any real consideration or concern for personal profit. These are the people who should be managing and developing the supply of water to everyone.

No-one says they have to work for free; but if they see their future selves living in a mansion, with a Ferrari in the drive, then maybe they should consider an alternative profession. And those who wish to do an honest day's work, and come home of an evening to a modest home, satisfied they have done their bit for Humanity, are ideal candidates for a job filtering Human waste out of the Elixir of Life.

And I think that once we identify that principle as sound, in relation to the provision of water, it shouldn't be too much of a stretch to extend it to Health Service Provision. We already do to it to those who wish to serve in the Police, Fire Service and the Military; why is the Health Service any different?

Again, I'm not proposing a Health Service run by slaves or serfs; I'm just saying that maybe Health Service Provision and Profit are not terms that naturally go together.

The totalitarian Communist states of the second half of the 20th Century created a phantom of uniform dullness and greyness, associated with social provisioning. No-one wants to drive around in shapeless cars, made of cow-dung, and fuelled by potatoes; but making Health Service Provision a socially funded, single-payer system, devoid of the magnificent profits enjoyed by large pharmaceutical corporations, is a long way from Communist drudgery.

And it need go no further. Over here you have Health Service and Water provision, possibly Education and even basic shelter; and over there, in the Free Market, you have everything else: fast cars; games consoles; high fashions; gourmet foods; and all the other trappings of a consumer lifestyle.

It really is about thinking in Colour, rather than just Black & White.
