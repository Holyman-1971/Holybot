When monetary currency was first used by humans, it was used to replace the bartering of goods and services. It became necessary because as human settlements expanded and came into contact with each other, particular goods and services were found to have different relative worth, depending upon the need for that good or service in a particular community.

Bartering works within a closed community, where only essential goods or services are being exchanged. For example, one sheep might be worth several bushels of wheat, because everyone in the community has a need for meat, wool and bread. The exact rate of exchange within a closed community is pretty stable, because there will likely be only one or two sheep farmers, and only one or two wheat farmers.

But when someone from a sheep farming community migrates, with his flock in tow, to a predominately wheat farming community, he suddenly becomes the Kingpin, because with his many disposable sheep, he can pretty much buy up all the wheat that is available, at the existing rate of exchange between sheep and wheat.

Furthermore, as goods and services became available that weren't strict necessities for Life, such as wine, jewellery, prostitutes etc., it became more difficult to establish the exact worth of those luxury items in relation to life essentials.

If one sheep could feed a family for a fortnight, and 10 bushels of wheat could do the same, then you have an obvious exchange rate. But how much is it worth to get pissed? Or look pretty? Or get laid?

So because of inter-community trade, and the rise in luxury goods and services, Civilisation developed the idea of Currency. A Currency System used an intermediate commodity as a token of exchange; and this was usually silver or gold, for the very good reason that these "noble metals" were pretty much impervious to degradation.

Precious metals, like Energy, can't really be created or destroyed (or at least couldn't easily be, in the pre-Industrial World): they just are.

So the earliest forms of currency were lumps of silver and gold that were subject to local exchange rate mechanisms. One sheep in a sheep farming community might only be worth 1 lump of silver within that community; but 10 lumps of silver in the wheat farming community. The sheep farmer could (and did) drive his flock to the wheat farming community to sell them (but so could other sheep farmers), and return not with 1,000 bushels of wheat, but 100 silver pieces.

Much easier.

Over time, currency became coin, initially out of the vanity of the ruler who minted the coin, but later as a way of making the limited amounts of silver and gold go further. As the years go by, coin generally had less and less actual valuable metal contained in the coin, and at that point, the ruler whose head was on the coin began to act more as a guarantee of the [b]*VALUE*[/b] of the coin, than its intrinsic [b]*WORTH*[/b].

For example, it was assumed that a coin minted with the head of a Roman Caesar on it, was more likely to contain the appropriate amount of gold and silver in it, than one carrying the head of some Goth chief you'd never heard of.

At the very least, if you did find yourself in possession of counterfeit currency that wasn't really worth what it was supposed to be, you could take it to the ruler (or his representatives) and question why he'd allowed his mark to be placed on a currency token with little or no actual worth. It was assumed that the stronger a ruler was, the less likely his currency would be counterfeited, and the more likely he would be to honour any shortfall in the actual value.

By the time we reach the 18th Century, the sums involved in mercantile exchange are such that the amount of coin required for large transactions, are becoming just as difficult - and a lot more dangerous - to transport, as a flock of sheep or 100 bushels of wheat. And so, largely thanks to the Italian Banking Families, the idea of the "Promissory Note" was born.

Here, rather than transporting the actual gold, one merely took a slip of paper that "Promised to pay the bearer, on demand, the sum of..." however much gold was involved in the transaction. If your promissory note carried the seal of one of the grand, post-Renaissance Banking Families (the Lombard's, the Rothschild's etc.), then you knew that what you possessed was worth far, far more than its weight in gold. Indeed, it was worth as much gold as it was [b]*WRITTEN*[/b] on the note that it was worth.

As the idea took off, a couple of things happened. The first was that these promissory notes began to be redeemable not just at the bank that issued them, but pretty much any bank that was a part of the overall banking system of the region. And the second was that these notes, particularly the low denomination ones, also began to be exchanged amongst the people who were holding them. And after a while, the bank note as we know it today was born.

Right up until the early decades of the 20th Century, it was still actually possible to take a bank note into a branch of the issuing bank - by this time, usually a Government Central Bank, like the Bank of England - and exchange it for its actual value in gold. But the necessity of governments to make bulk purchases from each other during World Wars One and Two meant that this facility (for ordinaries) was increasingly, and eventually permanently, suspended. The issuing governments simply didn't have the required amounts of gold available to honour the redemptions.

However, even though it wasn't possible to redeem the promissory notes, a "Gold Standard" was still in effect; meaning that the number of bank notes in circulation issued by a particular bank, still matched the amount of gold those banks theoretically held in their vaults.

After the Second World War though, many Central Banks - including that of the U.K., but [b]*NOT*[/b] including that of the U.S. - simply didn't have anywhere near the amount of gold required to cover all their bank notes in circulation. So a system similar to the Gold Standard was established by the Bretton Woods Agreements.

http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bretton_Woods_Agreement

Under this system, many countries (including the U.K.) fixed their exchange rates relative to the U.S. Dollar. The U.S. promised to fix the price of gold (at $35 per ounce), which meant that all currencies pegged to the dollar, also had a fixed value in terms of gold.

However, under the fiscal strains of its own War - Vietnam - the U.S. President Richard Nixon eliminated the fixed gold price in 1971, abolishing the Bretton Woods system.

From this point onwards, all currencies became [i]Fiat[/i] currencies. Instead of being backed by a nominal value equating to a transactionable commodity - gold - currencies became backed instead by an explicit government guarantee, or government [i]fiat[/i].

This [i]fiat[/b] stated that, "Whilst this currency in your hands might not be worth even the paper it is printed on, it [b]*DOES*[/b] represent the worth of the Government that issued it." So we've kind of come full circle back to the days of original coin currencies, only without the pretence of inherent value.

Basically, the currency in your wallet has value, because the Government that issued it [b]*SAYS*[/b] it has value.

Under the "conventional" system of Banking, in use really since the Renaissance, a depositor would leave money in a bank, in the hope that they could get it back when they wanted it. Or, indeed, send someone else around to collect it for them (if they had the right paperwork, of course).

And if the depositor agreed to it, the bank they left their money with would put it to work, lending it out to someone who wanted to borrow money. The bank would charge the borrower a higher rate of interest than it would pay the depositor, and pocket the difference.

And all was good, for a while.

Because of the high volume of deposit and withdrawal transactions in a normal bank, it could usually anticipate how much money it could expect to have at any one moment in its vaults: banks literally [b]*IMAGINE*[/b] how much money will be flowing in and out of their coffers. And because a bank could always [b]*IMAGINE*[/b] having more money than it usually had, it could always take the risk of lending out more money that it was actually in possession of.

In theory, if push ever came to shove, a bank could [b]*IMAGINE*[/b] the scenario whereby it called in all of its loans, in response to a theoretical situation whereby all its depositors asked for their money back at the same time. Though of course, very few banks ever [b]*IMAGINED*[/b] that [b]*ALL*[/b] of their depositors would ever ask for [b]*ALL*[/b] of their money back, at the same time. The banks [b]*IMAGINED*[/b] that their depositors [b]*IMAGINED*[/b] that their money would be better off in the banks vaults, earning interest, than stuffed under a mattress at home.

But just in case, banks usually took the view that it was prudent to never loan more than about 10 times the amount of money that they actually held on deposit... Just in case.
