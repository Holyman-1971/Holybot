So what are Credit Default Swaps?

Imagine being able to insure a car that you don't own or use.

Imagine it's the car your neighbours will let their teenage son drive, when he gets his license in a few weeks - and you know the kid is a reckless brat.

Now imagine that, by using financial derivatives called swaps, you can purchase as many insurance policies on this car as you can afford to pay premiums on.

When the brat eventually trashes and scraps the car, you - and any friends you clued in on the deal - might collect millions, even billions of dollars. By contrast, your neighbours, who bought real insurance on a real vehicle, get only its trade value paid out by the real insurance company.

This is basically what a Credit Default Swap is.

The buyer of the CDS makes periodic payments (the "Premium Leg") to the seller, and in return receives a payoff (the "Protection" or "Default Leg"), if the underlying financial instrument defaults.

The crucial difference between this and other, more usual forms of insurance, is that the buyer of the CDS [b]*DOES NOT NEED TO OWN*[/b] the underlying security (or other form of credit exposure). In fact, the buyer does not even have to suffer a loss from the default event.

This explains the primary problem with Credit Default Swaps. Anybody can bet on anything, so the nominal value of the bets [b]*FAR*[/b] exceeds the actual worth of any property or loans involved.

Still worse, no tangible or financial asset has to be in the picture. Wagers of any amount can be made, based only on opinions. You can bet on next Wednesday's weather, so long as there is a counter-party willing to take the other side.

The possibilities are endless.
