A few factual discrepancies:

1.  While the average worker may keep the same proportion of their salary in the US, UK and France, the GDP per capita is considerably higher at a national level compared between the US and France.  There was a recent study that put the State of Mississippi fairly high up among the EU in terms of per-capita GDP.

http://www.nemw.org/gsp1.htm

Actually it's California that's up there with the Western EU states, with a GDP/GSP of $1.5trillion, compared with the UK and France at $1.7trillion and Germany at $2.4trillion. Texas is kinda up there too with $900billion, but according to the data linked to above, Mississippi's GSP is only $76billion.

GDP per capita in France is only around $25k though, compared with $35k in the US. And from my own experience, I know that the cost of living is cheaper in most areas of the States too, though this must have a lot to do with the continued weakness of the Dollar versus the strength of the Euro.

2.  The US spends 3-3.5% of GDP on defense, not 30%.  The decimal point is important here.  30% of US GDP is about 3.6 trillion dollars, and we don't spend near that much on national defense.

Yup, I totally garbled the language on that. I'll rephrase :)

France spends about 30% of its GDP on Social Security. This is about $500 billion dollars, which is about what the United States earmarks for discretionary budget spending on "Defence". (Though of course, when you start to add in the supplementals... :))

Even during the height of the Cold War, we spent 4-6% of GDP on defense.  France spends about 2.5% of GDP on defense.  Of course "spending on defense" isn't quite burning the money on an altar, it's simply an allocation of funds to pay personnel and buy equipment and supplies from US and world suppliers.  It's a jobs program like any other.

Oh yes, that good old Military-Industrial complex that President Eisenhower warned America about...

3.  The effects of the Bush tax cuts are debatable.  What is not debatable is real GDP growth since the tax cuts and the decrease in unemployment even through disasters like Katrina/Rita and two years of precipitous oil price increases.  The US has long-term educational deficiencies (IMO) and other challenges to competitiveness that tax policy does not address, but the short-term recovery of the US economy has been quite strong and has handily outshone that of the EU in general, and France specifically.

It certainly looks good at first glance, and when you factor in the increases in "Defence" budgets, and the predominance of the "Defence" industry's contribution to American GDP, its easy to explain some of the growth.

But if you look at the figures for GDP growth during the 1990's, before Bush's tax cuts, annual percentage growth rates have actually slightly dropped since the tax cuts. To make those cuts justifiable, one would really expect to see higher growth rates. But then, Economics is complicated lace, and it is hard to measure the effects of the tax cuts against the backdrop of the DOT.COM bust and heightened security tensions since 9/11.

I think a real measure of the health of an economy has to be in the number of jobs being created. And whilst that has been some job growth under the Bush Administration, it has virtually all been in domestic, non-tradable jobs. High-tech, manufacturing and R&D jobs - which create exportable goods and services - have suffered a significant decline, not least because of the current penchant for "Off-Shoring".

4.  The idea that a company would hire, train and then discard a promising employee for someone they lack 23 months of observation of is pretty ludicrous, though your comments about live-to-work and work-to-live may have something to do with my perception, in that I would tend to value someone in a job situation for the work they can accomplish rather than the fact of their mere presence.

I think its about the nature of the job, and the relationship between employer and employee.

I totally agree that in professional, "white-collar" jobs, the employer is unlikely to sacrifice 23 months of expensive training, just to avoid employer liabilities. But in which case, the CPE legislation is irrelevant anyway. Employers taking high-calibre, graduate level trainees/apprentices to become the next generation of accountants, actuaries and architects, are unlikely to guide their employment policy by government stricture.

No, it is those unskilled, again domestic, non-tradable jobs that will be most affected. The so-called "McJobs", where actually only a single-day's training is intended to produce an employee who can do the job. These are jobs with high staff-turnover wherever they are in the World, yet in France it has always been a much more precarious situation for franchise-owners and operators to either let the employee go, or have the employee leave without fulfilling the social security obligations required under French Law.

In practice, if someone's not working out they'll be canned before the company wastes any more money on training or rehabilitation, not after 23 months of demonstrable shiftlessness -- the caveat being, that's how it would happen in an American company.  The question is how quickly French corporations will accept that they do have the ability to cut their losses and find a replacement, as this isn't a part of their thought process currently the application may, initially, be closer to your concept than mine.

I believe that as it stands at the moment, the French, like all E.C. states allows a 14-week probationary period, during which time they can serve notice on an employee without being encumbered by social security obligations.

It is hard to see why there is a need to increase this period from 14-weeks to 2 years. If you're an employer that needs to take 2 years to figure out whether someone is an appropriate and useful employee, then you probably deserve to go out of business...

5.  The pernicious influence of corporations on the government should be less prominent in France, where 54% of GDP comes from public sector industries, as compared to the US where 23% or so of the GDP is related to the public sector.  Instead, they suffer from the even more pernicious influence of public sector unions, as the spasm in Paris yesterday (and most of last week) demonstrates.

Actually I came across some information that surprised me just today. I always thought of France as a highly unionised society, but actually only about 8-9% of the French workforce belongs to a union; compared with around 16% in the States and 25% in the UK.

I think it also says a lot about media reporting bias that whilst all the protests are going on in France - I believe about 3 million marched a couple of days ago; the fact that 1 million public service employees staged a walkout in the UK this week has been largely unreported in the US media. Must be that "Special Relationship"...

Recent campaign finance reform here was effective in limiting free speech, but remarkably ineffective in limiting the influence of moneyed interests, what with Peter Lewis of Progressive Insurance and George Soros of "Short the Pound" fame managing to find ways to dump $45 million into 527 groups.

Oh yes, UK politics is currently undergoing one of its miserably regular "cash-for-access/privilege" scandals as well� Must be something in the air.

I find it quite amusing that when politicians get caught with the cash in their pockets, they immediately start mooting the possibility of state-funded campaign finance. I mean, they can�t get anyone to freely give them money because of the strength of their ideologies and manifestos; so their solution is to get the tax-payer to stump up the cash.

The simplest solution I can think of is to restrict party-political donations to card-carrying members and activists. If a political ideology can�t survive without prostituting the power and influence it might have its grubby hands on, then it deserves to wither on the vine of popular politics.

The only way to reduce the influence of corporations on government is to reduce what government controls.

Spoken like a *True* Conservative.

Rent-seeking is as old as time and highly-resistant to elimination, simply because neither the politicians nor those who are influenced by the politicians' decisions have any real interest in decreasing "speech" between the two groups, and since political donations are a form of speech, we have our present situation.

The answer has to be *total transparency* for our popular institutions and those that choose to serve in them.
