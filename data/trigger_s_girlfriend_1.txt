Hey Trigger, I think an important thing to realise is that you don't [b]*NEED*[/b] a valid reason to be upset.

That's kind of the whole thing about [b]*FEELING*[/b] upset: it's an Emotional experience; not a rational one.

If you are feeling upset because of what your GF is doing or saying, then you don't need to rationally justify [b]*WHY*[/b] you are feeling upset: you just need her to understand and acknowledge that you [b]*ARE*[/b] upset.

Now, what she chooses to do after she has understood and acknowledged your upset, is really the True measure of your relationship.

How important to her is this social engagement? Do her feelings for you take precedence over her feelings about her social life? Is there really no way for her to figure out a compromise that will allow her to hang out with her friends, without hurting the feelings of the person she is in a relationship with (the person she Loves?)?

Asking you to justify and rationalise the Emotional feelings of upset that you are experiencing, is a (almost certainly unconscious) way of putting herself in denial about the way her behaviour is affecting you; and by extension, denying you the right to feel whatever it is you are feeling.

I'm not ascribing any malicious or un-loving qualities to your girlfriend; I am quite sure that this particular issue just hasn't taken on the importance for her, that it has for you. For her, it is just a simple, superficial social engagement, that "you shouldn't be getting worked up about".

But for you, it has so many links into past Emotional turbulence and negativity, that even though you are aware that you might be responding unreasonably, you can't escape the fact that the situation is making you feel upset.

However, Emotions [b]*ARE*[/b] unreasonable Trigger - it's kind of the definition of the word: feelings are separate and distinct from reason.

But just because Emotions are unreasonable, doesn't mean that they are invalid.

It is unreasonable to think that Emotions aren't the most important aspect of a romantic relationship.

So, how might you handle this issue?

Well, I'm sure you have learned from your own Experiences that the handling of these kinds of issues within a relationship often - and usually very quickly - escalates into personal attacks and defensiveness. And that's the kind of thing that can act like a cancer within a relationship.

When you start asking each other to justify feelings and behaviours, the relationship takes on the hue of a courtroom battle, rather than a soft-focus, romantic association.

This goes for you as well as for her Trigger.

Just as she might be asking you to justify or validate the way you are feeling about what she is doing; you might be asking her to justify or validate the way [b]*SHE*[/b] is feeling about what she is doing.

Either way, the Loving aspect of your relationship is going to take a hammering, as you both focus on defending your own identities, by attacking each other's.

There are alternatives.

The psychologist Haim Ginott - http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haim_Ginott - the grandfather of effective-communication programs, recommended that the best formula for a complaint is "XYZ":

"When you did X, it made me feel Y, and I'd rather you did Z instead."

For example: "When you told me that you were going to go out with my old (male) classmate, and my ex-girlfriend, I felt unappreciated, hurt and angry. I would rather you had considered how I was going to feel, and perhaps have waited until we could all go out together."

This is a much better response than:

"You're a thoughtless, self-centred bitch, who doesn't give a fuck about me."

Open communication has no bullying, threats or insults. Nor does it allow for any of the numerous forms of defensiveness - excuses, denying responsibility, counter-attacking with a criticism and the like.

Try to hold onto the idea that Love and respect disarm hostility in a relationship.

A powerful way to de-escalate a fight before it can become terminal, is to let your girlfriend know that you [b]*CAN*[/b] see things from her perspective; and that her point of view [b]*DOES*[/b] have validity, even (and perhaps [b]*ESPECIALLY[/b]) if you do not agree with it yourself.

Obviously, don't take this position in the hope - or with the demand - that she will feel compelled to adopt the same position, less she morally lose the argument.

You are not trying to control or manipulate her: you are simply asking her to take care with your feelings.

And if she needs you to justify or validate why that is something she should do...

Then you probably [b]*SHOULD*[/b] ditch the bitch. :)
